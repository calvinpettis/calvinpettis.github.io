Introductory Post (8/20/2025)

My best in-class computing experience would probably be when coding started to click for me around CS1 or two. I did not know computers more than basic GUI operation when I first transferred here my junior year (I was a biology major at the time), and felt a little overwhelmed and confused in class. There was a constant feeling that I was always missing some important information that was supposed to be a given. I think it is when I saw a function that was a "boolean foo()" used as an input to a boolean variable, that the whole picture started fitting together more clearly and methods, returns, and functions started to make sense. Just like with any skill I have practiced, like exercise and learning a new instrument, results take time and practice and it is good to remember that consistency beats talent almost any day of the week.

The best out-of-class computing experience I have had was probably working on the data structures assignments. I would hit "run" on a solution of mine, and come back an hour later to see that it still had not solved the problem. When you got the solution to run in 0.1 seconds compared to 1hr+, it is very exciting to see the results of the hard work and correct algorithms at play. That class gave me a sense of how a project could handle scaling up if needed and has helped me optimize other assignments.

HiveTracks Case Study, Ethics (11/19/2025)

This was a great experience being able to see more of the internal workings of a tech company! I do not know many people in the field and have not worked any similar types of jobs, so it is eye opening hearing the experiences and laws and privacy regulatuions a company like that has to follow. Some things I took away from the experience:

  • There can be a lot of drama surrounding who actually owns the intellectual property of the code, things like using open source software limit your ownership which I was not expecting. Or having a company hold your code hostage!
  • Handling user data is a legal nightmare and having to cater to different countries' user protection laws is not easy. This company just has a clause in the privacy policy stating that EU users do not have GDPR protection and are subjected to the US's laws instead. I understand why you would do this though.
  • Security is even an issue in benign software like HiveTracks, with people hacking the site just to do it. Keeping user data safe is the #1 priority to a company like this, seeing as its entire business model relies on user data.

Final Blog Post (12/10/2025)

The three most important things learned this semester:

  • Privacy is not a given right and not respected much anymore, as shown by Edward Snowden and some of the class presentations on the upcoming upgraded surveillance era we are about to enter. The data is also hard to manage legally as shown by Dr. Wilke's company, Hivetracks.
  • Write good code, in most systems a bug does not matter. However, in the case of the Therac-25 and the MCAS systems of planes, this can cost lives if not written to a T. NASA also has coding guidelines to follow to make sure they do not send an astronaut into the sun.
  • Networking is important, the job market is really rough right now (Entry level job requires 3+ years of experience and hundreds of people apply to the same position) and if you do not know somebody at a company, your chances of getting in are slim unless you are very talented. Getting to the career fair and talking to employers helped a lot.
Final piece of advice to students coming into this course: It is rough out here, but don't be discouraged. Build projects, network (get on a software engineering discord and make friends, there might be somebody who could help out!), do your work, show up to class, you get out of this course what you put in.
